If your kid rides an e-bike or electric scooter in Wellington, the rules changed this fall, and tickets are now being written. The Village set a minimum riding age of 15, a photo ID requirement, a 15 mph speed limit on paths and new no-ride zones. Supporters say it's overdue after a wave of crashes across Florida. Critics, including the mayor, say an age limit is the wrong tool. Here's what the rules say, how we got here, and what both sides are arguing.

What the new rules cover

The Village Council approved two ordinances on August 11, 2026. They apply to all classes of e-bikes, electric scooters and other motorized "nonhuman-powered" micromobility devices on public roads, pathways and rights-of-way in Wellington. Golf carts aren't covered; they fall under a separate ordinance.

  • Age 15 and up. Florida has no statewide minimum age for e-bikes, so this is a local rule layered on top of state law.
  • Carry ID. CBS12 reported that a school ID counts only if it shows the rider's name, address and date of birth, so check your child's card.
  • 15 mph on paths. The limit applies on sidewalks and multi-use paths, where riders share space with walkers, joggers and horses.
  • Helmets under 16. Riders and passengers under 16 must wear a helmet that meets Florida law.
  • Banned areas. No riding in Wellington Town Center, neighborhood parks, on bridle and equestrian trails, or on the Heart Trail at Village Park during posted days and hours.

The rules took effect August 26. Written warnings started September 15, and citations began October 1. Check the Village's Micromobility Devices page for current fine amounts and maps of restricted areas.

Why the Village did it

Officials pointed to the rapid growth of e-bikes and scooters and a rise in crashes in Wellington and across Florida, including a fatal e-bike crash in Jupiter. Wellington isn't alone: in October 2025, the Town of Palm Beach banned e-bikes, scooters and similar devices from its sidewalks, trails and the Lake Trail.

The debate: safety rule or overreach?

The vote was 3-2, and the debate on the dais mirrored the one happening in Wellington kitchens.

The case for the age limit

  • A clear, enforceable line. Council Member Johnny Meier argued the Village needed a simple standard to protect public safety: "It is our job to make sure that our most vulnerable are safe." He asked whether parents would trade a child's freedom to ride for a neighbor's child being hurt.
  • Government has a role. Council Member Maria Antuna, who voted yes, pushed back on the idea that the Village shouldn't get involved: "I get very concerned when people say to us that government should not be involved in parent's lives."
  • Faster, heavier devices. Many of today's e-bikes and e-motos are far more powerful than the bikes parents grew up with, and they share narrow paths with pedestrians.

The case against

  • Punish reckless riders, not ages. Mayor Michael Napoleone called an age restriction "the lazy way to do this" and said the focus should be on reckless riding. Council Member Stephen Levin also favored education and enforcement over an age cutoff.
  • Age isn't the same as responsibility. Students who spoke at the meeting argued that education and helmets matter more than a birthday. "When you turn 15, you don't magically get responsible," one middle schooler told the council.
  • How kids get to school. For many middle schoolers, an e-bike is how they get to school and practice. Opponents said the rule shifts that burden onto parents' carpools.

What parents should know

  • Under 15? No riding a motorized device on public roads and paths in Wellington, even with a helmet.
  • 15 and older: make sure they carry a qualifying photo ID and, if under 16, wear a helmet.
  • Know the device. Under Florida law, an e-bike has working pedals and a motor of 750 watts or less. Higher-powered throttle machines often called e-motos may not legally count as e-bikes at all, which can bring registration and insurance requirements.
  • Liability is real. A local law firm's analysis notes that Florida law can hold parents civilly liable for damage caused by a minor's negligent operation of a vehicle, and some homeowners policies exclude injuries involving motorized vehicles.

Where do you land?

Is the age-15 rule a smart safety move or a step too far? Either way, it's now being enforced, so it's worth a family conversation before the next ride to school. For safer places to ride and play, see our guides to Wellington parks and things to do with kids.
