A billionaire, a beloved polo field, a private club for hundreds of families and a lawsuit against the Village. The fight over the Isla Carroll polo field on 120th Avenue South has become one of the biggest land-use battles in Wellington, and it raises a question many locals are now debating: did the Village protect the Equestrian Preserve, or did it turn down a deal that might have been better than the alternative? Here's what happened, what each side argues, and what could come next.

The fight at a glance

The land
About 79 acres at 3665 120th Avenue South, home to the Isla Carroll East polo field, in Wellington's Equestrian Preserve
Who wanted to build
McCourt Partners, led by billionaire Frank McCourt, and Discovery Land Company
The final plan
27 homes, an 8-acre private club for 325 families, a barn and equestrian complex, and the polo field preserved under a 45-year U.S. Polo Association lease
The decision
Denied by the Village Council in February 2026, with Vice Mayor Tanya Siskind the only supporter, and denied again in March
Now
The property owner sued the Village on March 26, 2026; the claims have not been decided in court

What was proposed?

McCourt Partners and Discovery Land Company, a developer known for private club communities, asked Wellington to rezone the Isla Carroll property from Residential Equestrian to an Equestrian Planned Unit Development. The version the Village's Equestrian Preserve Committee reviewed in January 2026 called for 40 homes on smaller lots plus a roughly 31-acre club area with an equestrian complex, sports facilities and membership capped at 300 families.

After pushback, the developers came back with a smaller plan. According to The Real Deal, the revised version included:

  • 27 homes, four of them on 2-acre lots meant for equestrian use, about a third less dense than the original
  • An 8-acre private club for 325 families with a gym, racquet courts, a pool, dining and retail
  • A 10-stall barn, an equestrian complex, bridle paths and shared paddocks
  • The polo field preserved for the U.S. Polo Association through a 45-year lease

The developers said cutting the number of homes cost them about $30 million. McCourt called the project "a passion project of mine."

How the Village voted

The plan struggled at every step. Wellington's Equestrian Preserve Committee recommended denial in October 2025, the Planning, Zoning and Adjustment Board was wary of the project, and in January 2026 the committee voted unanimously against the 40-home version. In February 2026, the Village Council rejected the revised plan in a late-night vote, with Vice Mayor Tanya Siskind the only member in support. A second written denial followed in March after the developers tried again.

Mayor Michael Napoleone said the project wasn't centered on equestrian use, which conflicts with the Village's comprehensive plan, and that the country club appeared to be the focus. The Village's attorney noted there is "no absolute right to a rezoning."

The case for and against

What critics said

  • Too much club, not enough horse. At committee hearings, critics argued that too much land went to clubhouse uses like a spa, dining and courts, and too little to permanent equestrian facilities. Some called the equestrian elements window dressing for a private club.
  • A precedent for the Preserve. Committee members worried that approving clustered homes around private clubs could gradually replace Wellington's traditional large-lot horse farms, and they pushed for a clear definition of the "equestrian lifestyle" the code refers to but never defines.
  • Practical questions. Speakers raised concerns about horse care, paddock space, bridle trail connections and traffic on 120th Avenue South.

What supporters said

  • It saves the polo field. The plan locked in the USPA polo field for decades, which ordinary lot-by-lot development wouldn't guarantee.
  • More access to horses. Supporters said a professionally managed club could give families without big private farms a way into the equestrian lifestyle, and pointed to Discovery's track record with high-end communities.
  • The alternative could be worse. Under current zoning, the owner can build up to 40 single-family homes on 2-acre lots, with no requirement to keep the polo field. The developers' attorney called their plan a "low-density project that complies with the equestrian lifestyle."

The lawsuit

On March 26, 2026, the property owner, 120th Avenue S, LLC, sued the Village in Palm Beach County Circuit Court, according to the Boca Post. The complaint argues that Wellington misread its own comprehensive plan by treating development in the Equestrian Preserve as needing to be primarily equestrian, says the Village adopted a new interpretation of its equestrian policies in February 2026, and points out that Wellington approved a similar mix of homes and equestrian features in early 2024. It asks a judge to order the Village to grant the rezoning. These are the plaintiff's claims and have not been proven in court.

Before the council vote, the developers' attorney also said they could pursue more than $100 million in lost property value and legal fees under Florida's Bert J. Harris Jr. Private Property Rights Protection Act, a state law that lets owners seek compensation when government action places an inordinate burden on their property.

Why this one matters

Isla Carroll lands in the middle of a bigger debate about how much the Equestrian Preserve should change. In 2024, the Council approved Mark Bellissimo's luxury communities and removed about 96 acres from the Preserve, now known as The Wellington. Supporters of the Isla Carroll plan see the denial as inconsistent; opponents see it as the Village finally drawing a line. Either way, the outcome of the lawsuit could shape what gets built on Wellington's remaining horse land for years.
