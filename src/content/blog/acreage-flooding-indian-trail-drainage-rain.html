About 20 inches of rain fell on The Acreage in a matter of days this fall, more than nine inches on Sept. 17 alone, leaving water standing in yards, swales and some streets. Leaders of the Indian Trail Improvement District (ITID), which runs drainage for the 110-square-mile area next door to Wellington, say the system did its job and no homes took on water. But one supervisor warns that growth in and around The Acreage is raising the risk of serious flooding, and the district is also in a dispute over a key drainage canal and short on money for promised road work. Here's what happened and what each side is saying.

Acreage drainage at a glance

The storm
About 20 inches over several days in September 2026, including more than nine inches on Sept. 17
The system
ITID maintains 164 miles of canals, 458 miles of roads and more than 900 miles of swales across about 17,000 lots
Homes flooded
None that officials knew of, according to ITID Executive Director Burgess Hanson
Canal dispute
ITID sued the Seminole Improvement District in March over use of the M-2 Canal, which drains much of ITID's west side
Road funding gap
About $3.31 million short of the roughly $6.36 million needed to finish the R3 Road Plan

"The system is working"

ITID leaders said much of what residents saw was drainage doing its job. Water collecting on a property isn't necessarily flooding, ITID President Elizabeth Accomando said, but a sign the system is moving water. She also said residents need to take more responsibility for keeping their own swales and culverts clear, the Town-Crier reported.

  • "There was just so much rain so fast… but our staff did an amazing job," Accomando said. "They worked from 6 a.m. until past dark."
  • "As far as I know, no houses got water in them," Hanson said.
  • Supervisor Richard Vassalotti said many calls came from newer residents who had never seen a big rain in The Acreage. "I don't call this a 'flooding event.' Most of the issues were minor," he said.

Why the risk is growing

Vassalotti also sounded a warning. Many of ITID's canals "were built 75 years ago, in the 1950s, when I suspect no one anticipated the kind of development we see out here today," he said. Larger homes on Acreage lots and nearby developments such as Avenir and Westlake have reduced the open ground that lets rain soak into the aquifer, he said, so the chance of serious flooding has gone up.

The M-2 Canal dispute

Much of ITID's west side drains through the M-2 Canal, which runs through the Seminole Improvement District (SID) and Westlake. In March, ITID sued SID to protect what it says are drainage rights dating to the mid-1960s. SID has asked for roughly $3 million in outfall structure work and about $300,000 a year for continued use. Hanson said the dispute didn't affect the September high water, that SID staff "communicated and coordinated with us very well," and that staff-level talks are underway to avoid more legal fighting.

The road plan shortfall

At its Sept. 16 meeting, ITID's board learned that finishing the last four segments of its R3 Road Plan, including 180th Avenue North, part of Tangerine Blvd. and portions of 73rd and 74th streets, would cost about $6.36 million, but only about $3 million remains from the 2022 bond issue. District engineer Jay Foy blamed inflation, early estimates made in 2020, and added work such as a school turn lane and speed tables. He suggested a scaled-back version of the Tangerine and 180th work near Frontier Elementary and Osceola Creek Middle. "This is something we could do now to get part of the promised improvements done," he said. Supervisors asked staff for detailed options before deciding.

The debate

  • The system held up. District leaders point to long hours by staff, no reported flooded homes, and a drainage network that moved an extraordinary amount of rain.
  • Neighbors have a role. Clear swales and culverts on private property are part of how the system works, Accomando said.
  • Old canals, new growth. Vassalotti's warning suggests the system built in the 1950s may need upgrades as development keeps coming, which raises the question of who pays.
  • Money is tight. Between the canal dispute and the road funding gap, ITID faces hard choices about what to fund now and what to scale back.

Why it matters for Wellington

Wellington shares the same heavy rains, flat land and growth pressures. For more on development and infrastructure out west, see the county transportation master plan and why Wellington's water bills are going up.
