Nearly a third of Wellington is set aside for horses. The Wellington Equestrian Preserve covers roughly 9,000 acres of farms, showgrounds, polo fields and bridle trails in the south and west of the village, and it shapes everything from zoning fights to the way you're expected to drive on Pierson Road. With the winter season about to start, here's a plain-English guide to what the preserve is, who makes the rules, where the trails are, and what both horse people and non-horse people should know.

What is the Equestrian Preserve?

The Equestrian Preserve Area (EPA) is a part of Wellington identified on the village's Future Land Use Map as horse country. The village describes it as about 9,000 acres in the western and southern portions of Wellington; its State of the Equestrian Preserve report puts the number at approximately 9,200 acres, or almost 30% of the village's developable land.

According to the village, the preserve includes:

  • Wellington International, home of the Winter Equestrian Festival
  • The National Polo Center
  • A network of public and private bridle trails
  • Hundreds of equestrian farms and facilities, from international show barns to family-owned farms with a few horses

The village counts more than 580 farms serving polo, dressage, hunter/jumper and recreational riders.

How Wellington became a horse town

The village traces its equestrian history to 1977, when polo arrived after the Gould company and its chairman, Bill Ylvisaker, acquired undeveloped parts of the Wellington planned community. Within a decade, the village says, Wellington was an international equestrian destination hosting the Polo World Cup, and other disciplines followed, including the Winter Equestrian Festival and Olympic jumping team trials.

Today the season runs roughly January through April. See our guides to the 2027 Winter Equestrian Festival and the 2027 polo season.

The Equestrian Preserve Committee was first set up in 2000. It's advisory, so its votes are recommendations, but they carry weight in land-use debates. Meetings are the first Wednesday of each month (subject to change), and agendas are posted on the village's Legistar page.

The bridle trails

The village's Equestrian Trails department maintains 15 named bridle paths: Black, Blue, Brick Red, Brown, Dark Green, Gray, Green, Greenbriar, Light Blue, Orange, Pink, Purple, Red, White and Yellow. A trail map is posted on the village's Equestrian Trails page and in its GIS Map Gallery.

How many miles? It depends which village page you read. The Equestrian Community FAQ says Wellington has established and maintained more than 100 miles of public and private bridle trails since 1999, running along roadway easements, canal rights-of-way and bridle path easements. The Equestrian Preserve page cites more than 57 miles. The difference likely comes down to what's counted, such as private paths.

Things to know:

  • They're multi-use. The village says the trails are designed for hiking, biking, running and riding. Give horses the right of way: slow down, give space and speak calmly so the horse knows you're a person.
  • No e-bikes. Wellington's e-bike ordinance bars riding them on bridle and equestrian trails. See our e-bike rules explainer.
  • Maintenance is in the budget. Trailheads, footing, markings, landscaping and road crossings are funded each year through the Capital Improvement Plan, with projects recommended by the Equestrian Preserve Committee under an Equestrian Trails Master Plan.

Driving near horses: what Florida law says

If you drive through the preserve, you will meet horses at crossings and along road shoulders, especially in season.

  • Use reasonable care. Florida Statute 316.0825 requires drivers to use reasonable care when approaching or passing someone riding or leading an animal on a road or shoulder, and prohibits intentionally startling or injuring the animal. A violation is a moving violation.
  • Slow down, pass wide and skip the horn. A spooked horse can throw its rider or bolt into traffic.
  • Watch the crossings. Some busy crossings, such as on Pierson Road, have flashing beacons. Stop when a horse and rider are waiting to cross.

Riders under 16 must wear a helmet on public roads, public trails and other public property under Florida Statute 773.06, with exceptions that include riding on private land.

The manure rules

With thousands of horses in season, manure is a real water-quality issue, because waste can wash into canals and lakes. Wellington's livestock waste rules require:

  • Covered, contained storage. Livestock waste must be containerized and covered, in a bin that keeps stormwater from carrying waste into nearby water. Bins need a sloped floor or a pad with a 2-inch curb and must meet setbacks from water bodies, wells and drainage easements, according to the village's inspection form.
  • Registered haulers only. Commercial haulers and self-haulers must be permitted and registered with the village every year, and waste must go to an approved disposal site.
  • No green bins. The village says it's against the law to put horse manure in vegetation carts.
  • Illegal dumping is a crime. The village notes that under Florida's litter law, commercial dumping can be a felony, and the sheriff's office enforces it.

Farm owners can request a bin inspection from the village online; inspections are done Monday through Friday.

The big debate: preserve or develop?

The preserve's boundaries are the most argued-over lines in Wellington. In 2024 the council voted to take about 96 acres out of the preserve for a luxury home project tied to new showgrounds, a decision we explain in The Wellington development explained. More recently, a proposal for homes on a polo field on 120th Avenue South drew strong opposition; see our story on the Isla Carroll polo field fight.

Those who favor holding the line say the preserve is what makes Wellington unique, that the horse industry is a cornerstone of the local economy, and that removing land once makes it easier to do again. Those open to change say landowners have property rights, that the showgrounds need investment to compete with newer venues elsewhere, and that well-designed projects can pay for equestrian improvements. The village's own stated goal is a year-round, world-class equestrian community built on partnership among residents, businesses and equestrian interests.
