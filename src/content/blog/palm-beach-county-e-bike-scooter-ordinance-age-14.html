After Wellington set its own e-bike and scooter rules this summer, Palm Beach County is now moving toward countywide-style rules of its own. On Oct. 20, 2026, county commissioners unanimously gave preliminary approval to a micromobility ordinance with a minimum riding age of 14, photo ID, speed limits on paths and a $64.50 fine, paired with a big push on education. Supporters say the rules respond to a wave of injuries among young riders. Others question whether more local rules are the right approach, after the governor vetoed a statewide bill over enforcement and surveillance concerns. Here's what the ordinance says and what each side has argued.

The county's proposed e-bike rules at a glance

Minimum age
14, with a government-issued photo ID (school IDs don't count because they don't prove age)
Speed
15 mph max on sidewalks, bike lanes, bike paths and pedestrian areas; 10 mph when a pedestrian is within 50 feet
Other rules
No wheelies, no riding on roads outside a bike lane or path, no riding that endangers others
Fine
A pedestrian citation of $64.50
Where it applies
Unincorporated Palm Beach County and county-controlled roads; not gated communities or private roads
Status
Approved unanimously on first reading Oct. 20, 2026; a second reading comes later

What the ordinance covers

The rules apply to Class 1, 2 and 3 e-bikes, motorized scooters and other motorized micromobility devices, the Town-Crier reported. Cities that have given traffic control authority to the county would be opted in automatically; others can adopt the rules on their own. Wellington already has its own rules, passed in August, with a minimum age of 15. For details, see our guide to Wellington's e-bike rules.

Why the county acted

  • Injuries are up. The Health Care District of Palm Beach County documented 613 injuries involving these devices from January 2021 to September 2026, and 59 percent of those injured were 12 to 15 years old, according to county staff.
  • Lots of emergency calls. Palm Beach County Fire-Rescue reported more than 600 calls for micromobility crashes from October 2025 to September 2026.
  • No statewide law. The Legislature unanimously passed SB 382 this year, which would have set a 10 mph limit near pedestrians and created a state safety task force. Gov. Ron DeSantis vetoed it in June 2026, leaving rules to local governments.

Education first

County officials stressed that tickets aren't the main goal. The plan includes $16,500 from the law enforcement trust fund for helmets and safety gear, a "Helmet Up!" school pilot, PBSO video and messaging campaigns, adult training classes, and outreach to HOAs.

The case for the ordinance

  • Safety on shared paths. "While these devices provide transportation and mobility benefits, they have also created increasing safety concerns on sidewalks, bicycle paths and shared public spaces," said Taruna Malhotra, deputy director of the county's Community Services Department.
  • Less confusion. "Different jurisdictions currently have varying rules, which creates confusion for riders and enforcement agencies," Malhotra said. Commissioners also said statewide action is still needed so the rules are the same everywhere.
  • Protecting kids. "When you're on this side of the dais, we have to do what's best to protect everybody in our community," Commissioner Joel Flores said. "I think this is one step that we're doing that."
  • Focused enforcement. "From a PBSO perspective, our enforcement is education," PBSO Lt. Ryan Mugridge said, adding that deputies will focus on "egregious complaints that are posing a risk to drivers and motorists."

The case for caution

  • Hard to measure. In vetoing SB 382, DeSantis wrote that a 10 mph limit within 50 feet of a pedestrian would be "difficult for a bicyclist to measure" while riding. The county's ordinance includes a similar rule.
  • Surveillance worries. The governor also argued that enforcing speed limits "will likely lead to enhanced surveillance by local governments against citizens," news outlets reported.
  • A patchwork remains. With a county minimum age of 14, Wellington at 15 and other cities setting their own rules, riders and parents still have to know which rules apply where, and the county rules won't apply on private or gated roads.
  • Infrastructure matters too. The Florida Bicycle Association, which supported the state bill, has said crashes are best prevented when policy, infrastructure and education work together, not rules alone.

What parents in Wellington should know

  • Inside Wellington, the village's rules apply: minimum age 15, photo ID, 15 mph on paths and no-ride zones.
  • If your child rides into unincorporated areas such as The Acreage, the county rules would apply once adopted.
  • Florida law already requires helmets for riders and passengers under 16.
