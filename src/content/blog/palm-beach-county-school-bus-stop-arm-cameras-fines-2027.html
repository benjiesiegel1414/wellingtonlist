Every Palm Beach County school bus is getting cameras that catch drivers who illegally pass a bus with its red lights flashing and stop arm out. Right now drivers who get caught receive a warning in the mail. Starting Jan. 5, 2027, it's a $225 fine. Here's how the program works, when you have to stop for a school bus (and when you don't), what happens if you get a notice, and the debate around these cameras.

What's changing

The School District of Palm Beach County and the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office are rolling out a School Bus Passing Safety Program with BusPatrol, a company that runs similar programs for school districts around the country. A 2023 Florida law allows school districts to use these "school bus infraction detection systems."

Each bus already had 10 interior security cameras, according to the district. The new system adds seven more on each bus: five on the outside and two inside, all facing out to watch traffic. The cameras turn on automatically whenever the bus shows its flashing red lights and extends its stop arm, and they record video and photos of any vehicle that keeps going.

Not every bus had the cameras when the warning period began, but the district says all of them will before fines start on Jan. 5.

How a camera ticket works

  1. The cameras record a vehicle passing the stopped bus while its stop signals are on.
  2. A deputy reviews it. BusPatrol forwards the footage to the Sheriff's Office, and a deputy decides whether it meets the legal criteria for a violation.
  3. The owner gets a notice by mail. If the deputy confirms a violation, the vehicle's registered owner receives it. Through Jan. 4, it's a warning. From Jan. 5, it carries a $225 civil penalty.
  4. You can review the evidence. CBS12 reports owners can watch the video and photos at AlertBus.com using the violation and license plate numbers on the notice, which also explains how to contest it or transfer it to the person who was driving.

CBS12 reports the camera fine adds no points to your license. That's different from a ticket written by an officer who sees you pass a bus, which is a moving violation (see the penalties below).

When you must stop for a school bus in Florida

This is where many drivers get confused, especially on Wellington's wide divided roads like Forest Hill Boulevard and State Road 7. Under Florida law and FLHSMV guidance:

RoadSame direction as the busOpposite direction
Two-lane roadStopStop
Multi-lane road with no median, or a center turn laneStopStop
Divided by a paved median or painted linesStopStop (painted lines and paved medians don't count as barriers)
Divided by a raised barrier, such as a concrete divider, or unpaved space at least 5 feet wideStopNo need to stop, but slow down and watch for children

When you're required to stop, stay stopped until the stop arm is pulled back and the road is clear of children. Drivers behind a bus going the same way must always stop.

Why the district is doing it

  • The scale of the problem: the National Association of State Directors of Pupil Transportation Services estimates about 40 million drivers illegally pass stopped school buses in the U.S. every year. CBS12 reports a single-day survey in Florida counted more than 8,000.
  • A huge route network: Palm Beach County buses cover 2,386 square miles a day and serve more than 18,624 bus stops, driving more than 13.2 million miles a year, according to the Town-Crier.
  • Crashes: FLHSMV figures cited by the Town-Crier show almost 3,000 crashes involving school buses in Florida in 2022.

Who pays for the cameras

BusPatrol installs the cameras at no upfront cost to the district, CBS12 reports. The program is violator-funded: of each $225 fine, the district gets $85 for school safety and BusPatrol gets $140 to cover its investment. Brevard County was one of the first Florida districts to adopt the system; WKMG News 6 reported that Brevard's cameras caught nearly 18,000 vehicles illegally passing buses in their first year.

Two views on bus cameras

Supporters say the cameras catch dangerous drivers that a single deputy never could, every notice is reviewed by a deputy before it goes out, and the warning period gives everyone time to learn the rules. They point out the fine only applies to drivers who break a law meant to protect children.

Skeptics raise questions about the for-profit revenue split and about accuracy. In Miami-Dade, the sheriff's office paused its BusPatrol program in 2025 after criticism that law-abiding drivers were ticketed, WLRN reported; it relaunched in May 2026 after the state clarified the rules. Some drivers have argued they were on a divided road where stopping wasn't required. That's why the right to review the video and contest a notice matters.

Tips for drivers and parents

  • Watch for flashing yellow lights: they mean the bus is about to stop. Slow down and get ready to stop.
  • Know your road: if there's no raised barrier or wide grass median, assume you have to stop in both directions.
  • Use the warning period: if you get a warning before Jan. 5, review the video and learn where you went wrong.
  • Parents: remind kids to wait for the driver's signal before crossing and to stay out of the "danger zone" right around the bus.
