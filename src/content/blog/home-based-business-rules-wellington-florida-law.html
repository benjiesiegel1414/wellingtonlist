Thinking about running a business out of your Wellington home, like tutoring, bookkeeping, hair, swim lessons or a small online shop? Since 2021, Florida law has sharply limited how much cities and villages can restrict home-based businesses. The issue came up again in September 2026, when the Royal Palm Beach Village Council discussed the rules after a local swim instructor asked whether she could teach from home. Supporters say the law protects small entrepreneurs. Local governments said it takes away a tool to protect neighborhoods. Here's what the law allows, what it doesn't, and both sides of the debate.

Florida's home-based business law at a glance

The law
Section 559.955, Florida Statutes, created by HB 403 in 2021
The big rule
Home-based businesses may operate in residential zones, and local governments can't regulate them more strictly than other businesses
Employees
People who work at the home must live there, plus up to two non-resident employees or contractors; remote employees are allowed
Must stay residential
The business has to be secondary to the home's use as a residence, and exterior changes must fit the neighborhood's residential character
HOAs
The law doesn't override condo, co-op or HOA declarations and covenants

What the law says

According to the enrolled text of HB 403, a business counts as home-based if it meets these conditions:

  • Employees: anyone working at the home must live there, except up to two non-resident employees or independent contractors. Additional employees can work remotely.
  • Parking: business parking can't exceed what "would normally be expected at a similar residence," and vehicles must be in legal parking spaces, not in the right-of-way or on unimproved surfaces. Local governments may regulate heavy equipment visible from the street or neighbors.
  • Appearance: exterior changes "must conform to the residential character and architectural aesthetics of the neighborhood."
  • Retail: retail transactions can't happen at a structure other than the home itself.
  • Nuisances: noise, fumes and similar rules can't be stricter than those for a home with no business.
  • Primary use: the business must be "secondary to the property's use as a residential dwelling."

Business owners who believe a local rule violates the law can challenge it, and the winning side can recover reasonable attorney fees and costs.

What happened in Royal Palm Beach

At its Sept. 17, 2026 meeting, the Royal Palm Beach Village Council took up the question after Councilman Richard Valuntas raised a swim instructor's concern that village rules wouldn't let her teach from home, the Town-Crier reported. Valuntas was interested in changing the code to allow it, but Village Attorney Mitty Barnard explained the state law already controls. "We, the village, cannot say you cannot do this," Barnard said. "We cannot enforce regulations that are against a home-based business that are more stringent than other businesses that are being regulated." Royal Palm Beach updated its code in 2022 to comply. The village directed the business owner to apply for a Business Tax Receipt showing how her business would meet the requirements.

The case for the law

  • Small businesses start at home. "Small business is the foundation of Florida's economy," Bill Herrle, Florida executive director of the National Federation of Independent Business, said when the bill was signed, according to The Capitolist.
  • One set of rules statewide. Supporters argued the law prevents a "patchwork quilt" of local rules that made it hard to know what was allowed from one city to the next.
  • Guardrails still apply. Businesses must still keep a residential look, follow parking rules, limit non-resident employees and keep the home as the primary use.

The case for caution

  • Local control. The Florida League of Cities opposed the bill as a preemption of local authority. Executive Director Jeannie Garner asked the governor to veto it, arguing it weakened cities' home rule powers and voided existing local ordinances, and calling zoning "an inherent function of local government."
  • Neighborhood impacts. The League had sought the ability to limit operating hours, signage, exterior storage, traffic and activity visible from the street, The Capitolist reported.
  • Costly challenges. Because winning challengers can recover attorney fees, local governments face financial risk if they try to enforce stricter rules.

What it means if you live in Wellington

  • Check your HOA documents first. Many Wellington neighborhoods have HOAs, and the state law doesn't override declarations or covenants that restrict commercial use.
  • Get the right paperwork. Home-based businesses are still subject to local business tax receipts. Contact the Village of Wellington and Palm Beach County for current requirements before you open.
  • Be a good neighbor. Keeping traffic, parking and noise at normal household levels is both the law and the easiest way to avoid complaints.

This article is general information, not legal advice. For your situation, talk to the village or a Florida attorney.
