If your home in Wellington, Florida backs up to a major road like Forest Hill Boulevard or Wellington Trace, a deadline is close: under the village's hedge and fence rules, non-conforming hedges along major thoroughfares must be replaced by Dec. 31, 2026. Here's what the rules require, which five hedge plants are approved, how fences must look, and how Wellington's code compliance process works, from a courtesy notice to fines and liens. We also cover parking, landscaping and other common violations.

The Dec. 31, 2026 hedge deadline

Wellington adopted its Hedges and Fences Ordinance (No. 2021-18) in October 2021 to create a uniform look along village thoroughfares. On July 16, 2024, the council adopted Ordinance 2024-02, which updated hedge heights, fence setbacks and hedge materials after residents raised concerns.

The village's fence and wall guidelines say all legal non-conforming hedges must be replaced by Dec. 31, 2026 with one of the five required hedge materials. That includes properties that back up to a major thoroughfare and don't have a fence or hedge at all.

Who's affected: properties whose rear or side property lines abut a major thoroughfare. The village's landscape FAQ lists Forest Hill Boulevard, Wellington Trace, Big Blue Trace, Birkdale Drive and Greenview Shores Boulevard among those roads; the full list is on Wellington's Major Thoroughfare Map (Figure 2 in Article 9 of the Land Development Regulations).

Hedge rules under Ordinance 2024-02

  • Height: hedges next to a major thoroughfare must be kept between 6 and 8 feet tall. Elsewhere, a hedge along a fence or wall must be at least as tall as the fence or wall.
  • Planting: 2 feet on center, at least 36 inches tall when planted. New hedges get two years to reach the required height.
  • Replace, don't patch: a hedge that's more than 30% dead, deteriorated or diseased, or that shows more than 18 inches of bare trunk at the base, must be fully replaced with an approved plant.
  • Irrigation and mulch: hedges along fences and walls need working irrigation, and the landscape bed along the thoroughfare must be mulched at all times.
  • Placement: fences, walls and hedges can't sit closer than 5 feet back from the front plane of the house on either side, unless the Planning, Zoning and Building Director allows it for things like A/C or pool equipment.

A note on older numbers: some of the village's FAQ answers still describe the original 2021 rules (an 8-foot hedge, planted 3 feet apart, with three years to grow in). The fence and wall guidelines page reflects the 2024 update, so check with Planning and Zoning at (561) 791-4000 if you see conflicting numbers.

Want to keep your existing hedge?

If your hedge isn't one of the five approved plants but is healthy and well kept, you can apply to the Architectural Review Board for an alternative material approval (the village has an Existing Hedge Material Waiver application). The village says code officers would focus on fence setbacks first, with hedge height and materials enforced over time.

Fence and wall rules

  • Setback: at least 3 feet from the property line, or from the roadway easement when next to a sidewalk or public right-of-way, to leave room for the hedge.
  • Hedge required: fences that face a road need a hedge on the outside, except where a gate is (and some fence types in the Equestrian Preserve Area).
  • Finished side out: the finished side of a fence or wall must face the street or the neighbor.
  • Privacy mesh: black, green or brown mesh is allowed on chain link or aluminum fences while the hedge grows in, but must come down within 30 days after the hedge reaches fence height.
  • Upkeep: fences can't lean or be held up by hedges. Clean or repaint when 10% or more of the surface is stained or 5% or more of the paint is peeling.
  • Repairs: a building permit is needed when damage exceeds 20% of a fence or wall section, and replacing more than 30% means the new work must meet current rules. See our building permits guide, including which small fence jobs may qualify for the new HB 803 exemption.

Landscaping basics

According to the village's landscape FAQ:

  • Every property needs landscaping, including shade trees, shrubs along the front and foundation, groundcover and grass. A bare front yard doesn't meet the minimum.
  • Most plants must be in the ground; only limited decorative pots count.
  • You may trim up to 25% of a tree or palm. Removing one requires a Tree/Vegetation Removal permit, including fruit trees.
  • You can trim a neighbor's branches that hang over your property line; the village suggests talking with your neighbor first.
  • Trash bins and mechanical equipment visible from the street must be screened. See our trash and recycling guide for cart rules.

Parking rules and tickets

Wellington code officers and Palm Beach County Sheriff's deputies no longer have to issue a warning first. The village prohibits:

  • Parking in a swale, on the street, or over most of a sidewalk
  • Overnight parking of a commercial vehicle not fully screened in a garage or behind walls and hedges
  • Recreational vehicles that aren't adequately screened (the code also regulates boats and trailers)

Tickets are $35, or $250 for a handicap space. With three or more unpaid regular tickets, or one unpaid handicap ticket, the state won't renew your vehicle registration. The village says swale parking damages drainage and can cause standing water, potholes and driveway cracks. Hosting a party? Call (561) 753-2560 ahead of time to arrange event parking without tickets.

Barking dogs and leash rules

Animal complaints in Wellington go to Palm Beach County Animal Care and Control at (561) 233-1200, not the village. County law requires dogs to be leashed or otherwise restrained off the owner's property, except in designated off-leash areas. For nuisance complaints such as habitual barking, the county investigates after receiving sworn affidavits from two unrelated residents in separate nearby homes. Cruelty and neglect can be reported anonymously. More in our dog-friendly Wellington guide.

How code enforcement works

  1. How cases start: citizen complaints come first, and officers also run proactive Neighborhood Improvement Inspections. The village says about 75% to 80% of cases come from those proactive inspections.
  2. No anonymous complaints: since a 2021 state law (Senate Bill 60), you must give your name and address to file a complaint, unless the violation is an imminent threat to health or safety or to sensitive habitat. Complaints are public records.
  3. Inspections: officers can't enter your property without permission; they inspect from the street, an easement or, with permission, a neighbor's property.
  4. Courtesy notice: usually a letter or door hanger giving you 3 to 30 days to fix the problem. It's a courtesy, not a requirement, and can be skipped for safety issues or repeat violations.
  5. Special Magistrate: if the violation isn't fixed, the case goes to a hearing. The magistrate can impose fines of up to $250 a day per violation, plus case costs typically $175 to $600.
  6. Liens: unpaid fines become a lien on every property the owner has in Palm Beach County. Liens unpaid for 3 months can be foreclosed, except on homestead property.
  7. Appeals go to Circuit Court within 30 days, not the Village Council.

Got a notice? The village's advice is to act quickly and call your code officer, especially if you need more time. In most cases, it says, extensions are given when circumstances warrant. Look up your officer on the village's Code Compliance Zone Map, and keep your case number handy.

Two views on stricter rules

Supporters say consistent hedges, fences and landscaping protect Wellington's look and property values, and the village says its goal is compliance, not penalties. On the other side, replacing an established hedge can be a real expense, and residents raised concerns about hedge height, fence setbacks and hedge materials under the original 2021 rules; the village says that's why the council adopted the 2024 revision. There's also an alternative-material option. If cost is a concern, ask the Architectural Review Board about keeping your hedge before you tear it out.
