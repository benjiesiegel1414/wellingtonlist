    What Amendment 3 Means for Palm Beach County Nonprofits and the Children's Services Council

    By Ben SiegelPublished November 25, 20266 min read

    Florida voters approved Amendment 3 on Nov. 3, 2026, raising the homestead exemption on non-school property taxes. For homeowners in Wellington and across Palm Beach County, that means tax savings starting in 2027. For local nonprofits, many of which depend on property-tax-funded agencies such as the Children's Services Council, it means planning for less money. Here's what local leaders are saying, the numbers involved, when the changes hit, and the case on both sides.

    Why nonprofits are worried

    Amendment 3 raises the homestead exemption on non-school property taxes, which lowers the taxable value of homes for cities, counties and special taxing districts. Several of those districts pass much of their money on to local charities. In Palm Beach County, two big ones are the Children's Services Council (CSC) and the Health Care District.

    The Children's Services Council is a special taxing district that funds programs for children and families. Its CEO, Dr. Lisa Williams-Taylor, said in a statement to the Town-Crier that the council relies on property taxes for 90% of its budget and anticipates a 14% budget reduction, about $26 million, by 2028-29. "This reduction will no doubt be felt by our organization and, in turn, the funded programs and families we serve," she said.

    The CSC estimates each household contributes about $166 a year to the council, based on an average home value of $350,000, and Amendment 3 could eliminate much of that over time, the Town-Crier reported. CBS12 reported that the CSC helped fund about 4,000 summer camp scholarships this year, at a cost of up to $4.5 million, and said it was too early to know whether scholarships would be cut.

    The meeting in Wellington

    Before the vote, the Nonprofit Chamber for Health and Human Services held a presentation on the amendment's community impact on Oct. 26 at the Wellington Community Center. WPTV reported that nearly 100 people attended.

    Michelle Gonzalez, president of the 80-member chamber and CEO of the Healthy Mothers, Healthy Babies Coalition of Palm Beach County, told the Town-Crier that nonprofits are working through contingency plans. Her own organization gets 62% of its funding from the Children's Services Council. "There are many unknowns," she said.

    Other nonprofit leaders described a ripple effect. The CEO of the Center for Child Counseling, which serves 7,500 children a year and relies heavily on CSC funding, told the Town-Crier: "If their funding shrinks, then all our programs shrink." The CEO of the Milagro Center, which runs after-school, summer and arts programs for low-income families, said her board has already asked for a backup plan, and that "fundraising will become a much larger part of social services."

    Savings and cuts are estimates. Actual amounts depend on home values, tax rates set each year, and the choices local boards make.

    When the changes hit

    • 2026 tax bills (the ones mailed this November) aren't affected. See our property tax bill guide.
    • 2027: the non-school homestead exemption rises to $150,000.
    • Oct. 1, 2027: health and human services funding is unaffected until the current budget cycle ends, the Town-Crier reported, so nonprofits aren't expected to feel a real pinch until the 2028 budget cycle.
    • 2028: the exemption rises to $250,000, then adjusts for inflation.

    Over the coming months, the CSC says it will work with its board on "how best to balance this constitutional mandate with the needs of local children and families." Local governments, including Wellington, will build their 2027-28 budgets at public hearings next summer and fall.

    What it means for Wellington

    Gonzalez told the Town-Crier it's too early to know which organizations will be hit hardest, and that smaller, less wealthy cities such as Belle Glade could feel the impact more than larger, wealthier ones such as Wellington and West Palm Beach. Wellington homeowners will see savings on the village, county and special district lines of their bills, while services that families here use, from summer camp scholarships to counseling and prenatal programs, rely partly on the same tax base. For the village's own budget choices, see why Wellington didn't lower its tax rate.

    The case on both sides

    Supporters say Amendment 3 gives homeowners real relief as housing, insurance and living costs climb. Florida Realtors, the measure's main backer, contributed $18 million to the Vote Yes on 3 campaign, WLRN reported, and its CEO said the amendment is "about giving Florida homeowners actual meaningful tax relief." Supporters told CBS12 it would help seniors on fixed incomes stay in their homes, pointing to state data showing nearly 30% of Florida homeowners 65 and older spend at least 30% of their income on housing, and that it pushes local governments to focus spending on core services. The lower 5% cap on non-homestead assessment increases, they argue, gives landlords predictability that could help stabilize rents.

    Opponents and nonprofit leaders say the savings come at the cost of services families depend on. "This is not a tax decrease; it's simply a tax shift," County Administrator Joe Abruzzo told CBS12, saying the county would have to cut spending, raise fees or shift more of the burden to non-homestead property. Nonprofit leaders also warned that private donations can't easily fill the gap, since many groups will be chasing the same donors.

    Both sides agree that the details now depend on budget decisions by local boards over the next two years.

    Frequently asked questions

    How does Amendment 3 affect the Children's Services Council of Palm Beach County?

    The CSC, which gets about 90% of its budget from property taxes, projects a 14% budget reduction of about $26 million by 2028-29, its CEO told the Town-Crier.

    When will Palm Beach County nonprofits feel Amendment 3?

    Health and human services funding is unaffected until the current budget cycle ends on Oct. 1, 2027, so the real pinch is expected in the 2028 budget cycle, the Town-Crier reported.

    How much does Amendment 3 save homeowners?

    The non-school homestead exemption rises to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. Florida Realtors estimated average savings of about $1,000 in 2027 and $1,800 in 2028; actual savings depend on home value and local tax rates.

    What percentage did Amendment 3 get?

    Amendment 3 received 61.98% of the statewide vote (4,922,400 votes), above the 60% needed to pass, the Town-Crier reported.

    Does Amendment 3 affect school taxes?

    No. The larger exemption applies to non-school property taxes, so school district taxes are not affected.
