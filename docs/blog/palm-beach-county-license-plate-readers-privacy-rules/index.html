Palm Beach County Takes a Closer Look at License Plate Readers

By Wellington List staffPublished October 29, 20264 min read

License plate reader cameras are a familiar sight on poles across Palm Beach County, and now the Palm Beach County Commission is taking a closer look at how they're used. At a Sept. 29, 2026 workshop, commissioners asked the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office (PBSO) to answer questions about what the cameras collect and how the data is protected. Supporters say the readers are a valuable tool for catching criminals, with safeguards already in place. Commissioners worried about privacy want clearer answers first. Here's how the county's rules work and what each side has said.

License plate readers in Palm Beach County at a glance

What they do
High-speed cameras capture license plate images and convert them into digital data that law enforcement can use in investigations
County approvals
About 50 locations in county rights-of-way are in place or approved for installation
Who can get a permit
Only public police or sheriff's departments, and only for official law enforcement duties
If rules are broken
Permit holders have 30 days to remove the cameras or face a fine of $1,000 per day
Status
Commissioners asked PBSO to answer their questions before deciding whether any changes are needed

How the county's rules came about

In 2019, law enforcement asked the county for permission to install the readers in county rights-of-way, according to a staff presentation reported by the Town-Crier. Commissioners authorized permits with conditions, including that the cameras be used solely for law enforcement purposes. No permits were actually issued until 2022. Several cities and villages have also installed readers within their own boundaries.

The review comes amid national reports about possible misuse of the technology. Camera makers including Flock, Axon and Genetec have been at the center of those debates, and in Florida, Gov. Ron DeSantis recently ordered Flock cameras removed from state rights-of-way, the Town-Crier reported.

The question of what can be shared

In September, county officials asked every license plate reader permit holder for contract and data information. PBSO said it couldn't provide certain information publicly and suggested briefing commissioners individually or in a closed-door "shade" meeting. The county attorney's office advised that the request didn't meet the legal requirements for a shade meeting. "We haven't been personally made aware of any incidents with our permits, but we have read the headlines and seen some other cases," County Engineer Joanne Keller said.

The case for the cameras

  • An investigative tool. Plate readers help law enforcement track vehicles tied to crimes, and even skeptical commissioners acknowledged their value. "It's a great tool for you guys to apprehend people who do things the wrong way," Commissioner Joel Flores told PBSO.
  • Guardrails already exist. PBSO Major Thomas Fresneda said the agency runs weekly random audits looking for anomalies. "We have guardrails in place to make sure that, to the best we can, the system is not being misused," he said. "We've identified no misuse within the system."
  • Targeted placement. Fresneda said PBSO mainly installs readers around critical infrastructure related to dignitary protection, with the rest within municipalities, and that there are "many misconceptions" about the technology.
  • Strict permit terms. County permits already limit the cameras to police and sheriff's departments and carry steep daily fines for violations.

The case for caution

  • Privacy for law-abiding drivers. Flores asked whether the cameras collect data beyond plates, such as who is driving and what they look like. "I don't think I should compromise my privacy for people who break the law," he said.
  • Unanswered questions. Fresneda wasn't able to give definitive answers to many of Flores' questions at the workshop, and commissioners asked PBSO to follow up.
  • Transparency. PBSO's position that some contract and data information can't be shared publicly left the commission without a full public picture of how the data is handled.
  • A broader trend. The county is one of a growing number of local governments rethinking plate readers after national reports of misuse.

What happens next

Commissioners are waiting on PBSO's answers. Depending on what they learn, they'll weigh residents' privacy against the readers' value as an investigative tool. For how a neighboring city is using similar technology, see Westlake's speed cameras and license plate readers, and for Wellington's own camera program, one year of school zone speed cameras.

What do you think?

Do license plate readers make Palm Beach County safer, or do they collect too much information about everyday drivers? What rules would make you comfortable with them?

Frequently asked questions

How many license plate readers has Palm Beach County approved?

About 50 locations in county rights-of-way have license plate reader approvals that are either in place or approved for installation, according to a county staff presentation in September 2026.

Who can get a permit for a license plate reader in county rights-of-way?

Only public police or sheriff's departments, and only to support their official law enforcement duties.

What happens if a permit holder breaks the county's rules?

The permit holder has 30 days to remove the license plate readers or faces a fine of $1,000 per day.

Has PBSO found misuse of its license plate reader system?

PBSO Major Thomas Fresneda told commissioners the agency runs weekly random audits and has identified no misuse within the system.
